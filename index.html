The problem we solve

Launching from Earth is fighting gravity.

Every kilogram of propellant used beyond low-Earth orbit costs more than its weight in gold. The Moon changes the equation: 1/6 the gravity, no atmosphere, water locked in the regolith. Refueling in lunar orbit reduces the cost of reaching Mars by an estimated 60%.

Our approach

Mission architecture
in four stages.

01
EXTRACT

Autonomous rovers excavate regolith from permanently shadowed craters at the lunar south pole, where water ice concentration reaches 5–8% by mass.

Thermal desorption and microwave heating sublimate trapped water ice from regolith at temperatures below 100K. Autonomy stack derived from Mars surface mission heritage. Target: 1 tonne/day water production by 2036.

02
PROCESS

Electrolysis splits extracted water into liquid hydrogen and oxygen. Power from a co-deployed fission surface power system — 10 kWe continuous, no solar dependency.

Proton exchange membrane electrolysis. Vacuum environment reduces refrigeration load. Waste heat from fission power used for regolith processing, increasing overall energy efficiency to approximately 68%.

03
LIQUEFY

LH₂ stored at 20K, LOX at 90K. Cryogenic tanks use multilayer insulation with regolith overburden for radiation shielding. Zero boiloff is the hard engineering challenge.

Passive and active cryocooling. Sunlit surfaces provide thermal gradient for passive cooling. Active cryocoolers handle residual heat loads. Storage lifetime target: 180 days without significant boiloff.

04
DELIVER

Propellant launches to low lunar orbit via a dedicated ascent vehicle, transferring to customer spacecraft at the depot. No lunar spaceport required at pilot scale.

In-orbit cryogenic propellant transfer in lunar gravity. Partnering with two launch providers developing cryogenic depot capabilities. First transfer demonstration targeted for 2034.

Technology pillars

What this actually requires.

Honesty is the feature. We list the hard parts because understating them is how projects fail.

01
ISRU Excavation

Extracting water ice from permanently shadowed regions requires robotic systems operating at −200°C, with zero solar power, in regolith unlike any Earth analog. Cutting forces, dust abrasion, and thermal cycling degrade mechanisms on timescales of weeks. Our timeline requires years.

Challenge: Severe
02
Cryogenic Storage

Storing liquid hydrogen at 20K for months on the lunar surface — in hard vacuum, with thermal inputs from regolith contact and adjacent solar-illuminated terrain — is an unsolved engineering problem. Zero boiloff requires active cryocooling with a power budget that does not yet close at pilot scale.

Challenge: Severe
03
Autonomous Robotics

Round-trip communication delay to the lunar south pole reaches 2.6 seconds at minimum. Full autonomous operation is required. Terrain navigation, fault detection, and self-repair — in an environment with no GPS, partial communication windows, and electrostatically charged abrasive dust.

Heritage: Mars 2020, Curiosity, VIPER
04
Nuclear Surface Power

Permanently shadowed regions receive no solar power by definition. Electrolysis and liquefaction require continuous kilowatt-scale power. Fission surface power is the only viable solution — and no civilian fission reactor has operated on the lunar surface.

Heritage: SNAP-10A, NASA Kilopower

Open problems

What we haven't
solved yet.

Transparency is faster than hype. These are the real technical challenges standing between now and a functioning lunar propellant economy. We publish them because the people who can help are reading this.

Problem 01
Long-duration cryogenic hydrogen storage in vacuum
Zero-boiloff LH₂ has been demonstrated in Earth laboratories and for short LEO durations. No 180-day lunar-surface storage has ever been demonstrated. Passive MLI loses effectiveness when support structures conduct heat through the insulation system.
Our bet: active cryocooling at 10–20 W/tonne with a novel support structure that minimizes parasitic heat load. Candidate design exists. Not yet tested in relevant environment.
Problem 02
Autonomous excavation inside permanently shadowed regions
No robotic system has operated inside a PSR. Temperatures reach 40K. There is no sunlight. Radar and LiDAR sensors have not been validated at these temperatures. Orbital terrain models have ~1m resolution — insufficient for autonomous navigation.
Our bet: structured-light and thermal IR sensing can provide navigation in PSR darkness. Funding a terrestrial test campaign in cryogenic vacuum chamber analogues.
Problem 03
Lunar dust contamination of long-duration mechanical systems
Apollo-era data confirms lunar dust is electrostatically charged, sub-micron, and highly abrasive. It penetrated every sealed system on every Apollo surface mission. No mechanism has operated on the lunar surface for more than ~12 days. Our timeline requires continuous operation over years.
Our bet: contactless magnetic drive mechanisms and electrostatic dust mitigation panels. Mars rover heritage points toward enclosed systems. We do not have a proven multi-year solution.
Problem 04
In-orbit cryogenic propellant transfer at commercial volumes
Cryogenic fluid transfer in microgravity has been demonstrated at small scale. No LH₂ transfer at commercial volumes in lunar orbit has been attempted. Settling propellant in lunar gravity requires careful sequence planning.
Our bet: dedicated depot architecture with settled transfer using minimal thrust, borrowed from Starship tanker concepts. Monitoring SpaceX cryogenic transfer demonstrations closely.
Problem 05
Regulatory framework for commercial lunar resource extraction
The Artemis Accords provide a non-binding framework. No binding international treaty governs commercial lunar extraction. Ownership of extracted resources remains ambiguous under the 1967 Outer Space Treaty.
Our bet: the regulatory window will consolidate around 2028–2030 as Artemis missions create precedent. Contributing to policy through the ISRU Working Group at IAC.
